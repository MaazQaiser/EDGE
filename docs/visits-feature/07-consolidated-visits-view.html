FilterGo · Visits · Design spec

Who are we visiting, and when are they due again?

The consolidated visits view was demoed as a week of calendar and rejected outright. This is the redesign: a Companies tab in the scheduler that answers a customer’s question in one screen and a year in one scroll — built on the visits data that already exists, and leaving the Visits tab exactly as it is.

The brief

What the rejection actually said

The consolidated view was asked for by Brian Gessink and Jason, through Mehroz, so that a franchisee would stop drilling into individual sites to find out what was happening. What was demoed showed the current week, in calendar form. Umair rejected it in one sentence — “not helpful at all, this doesn’t work” — and then reframed the problem in a way that is worth separating into its three distinct claims, because they fail for different reasons and only one of them is about the calendar.

  • The horizon is wrong. Filters are replaced quarterly. A week-at-a-time surface makes “when is this customer next due” a twelve-click question. The unit of the customer’s question is a year; the unit of the demo was a week.
  • The subject is wrong. “The scheduler shows a run sheet, and you must click into it to discover who you’re visiting.” The rows are routes. The question is about companies. Every row in the scheduler is an answer to a question the franchisee is not asking.
  • The form is wrong for this job. A calendar answers “what happens on this date” — it is a planning instrument, and it is indexed by date. A list answers “when does this thing happen next” — a lookup instrument, indexed by subject. Quarterly service is a lookup problem. As Umair put it, a calendar is “not the regular customer experience.”

None of that makes the week grid wrong. It was built against session 2’s problem statement — is all of this week’s demand visible, and is it routed? — and it answers that well. The mistake was offering one instrument for two jobs. So the Visits tab is not touched, not re-defaulted and not hidden: this is a new tab beside it, reading the same data. The planner keeps the grid they have; the franchisee gets a surface that was never there.

The moment this is for

A customer calls: “when are you next coming to the Greenwich building, and what about the other three?”

Today: open the scheduler, page through weeks, open each run sheet, read its stops, hope you recognise the site names. After this: type the company name, read four dates.

The model

Subscribe & Save, with filters instead of coffee

Umair named the reference and the reason: “we don’t have to reinvent the wheel… I am not above copying somebody.” Amazon’s Subscribe & Save solves the identical problem — a recurring delivery on an interval the customer half-remembers — and it solves it without ever drawing a calendar. Worth being precise about what carries over, because the mapping is almost one-to-one.

Subscribe & SaveFilterGoWhat it buys us
A subscriptionA property’s recurring service The stable object the list is built from. Not the visit — the arrangement that produces visits.
“Every 2 months”, in words“Every 3 months”, in words Cadence stated as text, so the interval never has to be inferred from the gaps between dates.
Next delivery date, as textNext visit date, as text The single most-wanted fact, at the top, unclickable. Reading it is the whole interaction.
“Your upcoming deliveries”The company’s next 12 months A forward list, not a grid. Scroll replaces navigation.

“Estimated” until it shipsProjected until it is scheduled The load-bearing one. Amazon is honest about which dates are firm; so is this. See Planned and projected.

What does not carry over: Amazon’s user has eight subscriptions and one address. A franchisee has hundreds of properties across dozens of companies, on five different intervals, some of them not scheduled at all. That is why the design below adds a company rail and a period roll-up on top of the Subscribe & Save list — the list itself is copied almost verbatim.

Structure

One dataset, two views

The agreed design is two views of the same data: a period roll-up — today, this week, this month — listing companies and the properties being visited; and a company schedule — one company, its full year. They are not two screens. They are one screen whose left rail is the list of companies and whose pane is whichever answer you asked for. Selecting a company is the switch between the two views, which is exactly what “left side = company names, then their visits underneath” describes.

Where it lives

A new Companies tab in the scheduler’s own tab row, beside the ones already there. On Filter Go that reads Filter Replacement Service · Visits · Companies; on a multi-service tenant it lands after Visits in the same position. Nothing about the Visits tab changes — not its default, not its views, not its toolbar.

The tab row is the right home because that row already answers what kind of thing am I looking at — a service, its demand, and now the people that demand belongs to. It is not a page under the nav, because everything this view needs is scheduler chrome: the same filters, the same status vocabulary, the same footer counts, and a franchisee who should not have to learn that visits live in two different menu items.

The period roll-up

What each line carries

ElementContents and rules
Scope control Today · This week · This month, with the resolved range printed next to it in words. “This month” is the calendar month, because that is how a customer says it; the range text removes the ambiguity either way.
Overdue band Missed and past-due visits pinned above everything, in the alert palette — the same device as the unassigned band on the week grid. A visit that did not happen outlives its date (06 D5), and a customer-facing list that quietly drops it is a list that lies.

View B

The company’s year

Select a company and the pane becomes its full forward schedule: every property, every date it is due over the next twelve months, filterable down to one location. The requirement states the volume exactly — “next 4 visits quarterly, or next 12 if monthly” — and that is not a setting anyone has to choose. It falls out of the cadence: twelve months of a quarterly service is four dates, twelve months of a monthly service is twelve.

The twelve-row rule

A quarterly property produces four rows and a monthly one produces twelve. A fortnightly property produces twenty-six, and a weekly one fifty-two — which would bury the other properties under a single noisy building. Each property shows at most twelve dates, then collapses to “+22 more through Mon 2 Aug 2027”. The cap is set at twelve deliberately: it is exactly the monthly case, so the stated requirement is met verbatim, the pathological cases are bounded, and nobody has to choose a number.

History gets one line — Last visit Mon 3 Aug · completed — and no more. This view faces forward; the full service history already exists on the site’s own Schedule tab and duplicating it here would double every block for a fact that is rarely the question.

The honesty rule

Planned and projected are not the same fact

This is the part the design has to get right, and it is the part a year-long list makes unavoidable. Visits exist as real objects only inside the window the scheduler has actually generated. Beyond it there is no visit — there is a cadence, an anchor date and a multiplication. A twelve-month list therefore mixes two kinds of date, and they must not look alike.

PlannedProjected
What it isA visit record — a hit with an idCadence × interval from the last anchor
MarkerFilled dot, full-strength inkHollow dot, ~70% ink, the word expected
CarriesTime window, route, technician, statusA date. Nothing else.
ClickableYes — opens the visit drawerNo. There is nothing to open.
Can moveOnly by someone moving itFreely, as earlier visits shift
Why this is worth the pixels This list will be read down a phone to a customer. A date that reads as firm and then moves costs more than an estimate that was labelled as one — Amazon learned this and marks every unshipped delivery “estimated”. It is also the same principle session 4 applied to the demo data when a routed visit whose window had passed was made to resolve to missed (06 D11): the screen is not allowed to assert something the system does not know.
Dependency this creates Projection needs the contracted service interval to be a stored, per-property field, and it needs the backend to state how far ahead visits are actually generated so the boundary can be drawn. The interval was already an open question in the handoff, where session 3B narrowed it to a compliance nicety — “is this site overdue against its contract”. This view promotes it back to a hard dependency. Without it the year is guesswork; without the horizon field the planned/projected line cannot be drawn and every date has to be labelled as an estimate.
Interaction

What is clickable, and what is only text

“Dates are info text only, not clickable” is an explicit requirement, and it is a good one — it is the requirement that stops this becoming a calendar by other means. But a screen where nothing responds is its own failure, so the affordances are placed deliberately and sparsely.

TargetBehaviour
A dateInert. No link, no hover, no cursor change, on either kind of date.
A planned visit rowOpens the existing visit drawer, with the state rules already settled in 06 — completed is read-only (D4), past is read-only except missed (D5), a started route accepts an insert but marks it (D3).
A projected rowInert, with a tooltip stating where the date came from: “projected from a 90-day cadence · not yet scheduled”.
The Unassigned pillThe one action this surface carries. Opens the same add-to-a-route flow the grid uses. Seeing unrouted demand and being unable to act on it is the exact frustration the visits tab was built to end.
A property nameOpens the site, on its Schedule tab. Names are navigation; dates are not.
A company in the railSwitches the pane to that company’s year. Clearing it returns to the period roll-up with the previous scope intact.
ExportDownloads the visible company year as CSV. Download only — no send, no email. Handing a schedule to a customer is the franchisee’s conversation to have.
Everything elseNot here. Bulk selection, harmonize and route building stay on the Visits tab, which is the reason that tab keeps its own shape. This is a read surface, and the discipline is what keeps it fast to scan.
Decisions

D14 – D24

Numbering continues 06’s sequence, so the visits record stays one list. Rows marked in the alert colour change or reverse an earlier decision for this surface only — the grid keeps its own behaviour and the reason is stated in each case.

ID
Decision
Why
D14
A new Companies tab in the scheduler tab row. The Visits tab is untouched — same default, same views, same toolbar.
Two jobs, two instruments — but the second instrument gets its own place rather than displacing the first. Revises this document’s first draft, which made the list the Visits tab’s default view: re-defaulting a working screen to solve a different problem taxes the planner for the franchisee’s benefit, and nobody asked for that trade.
D15
One screen: a company rail plus a pane. Selecting a company is the switch between the two views.
“Left side = company names, then their visits underneath”, taken literally. Two separate screens would need a navigation the requirement never asked for.
D16
A company is keyed on customerId and displayed from site.company. Properties with no company group under No company on file.
Two customers can share a name and a name can be edited. Dropping the orphans would hide demand, which is the failure this whole tab exists to prevent.
D17
The year is property-major, not month-major.
The question is “when is this building due”. Month-major makes you scan twelve headings for a name you already know.
D18
Visit count per property is a consequence of cadence over a fixed 12-month horizon — capped at 12 rows, then collapsed.
Delivers “4 if quarterly, 12 if monthly” verbatim, bounds the fortnightly and weekly cases, and asks nobody to pick a number.
D19
Projected dates are marked, carry no route and no status, and are inert.
They are arithmetic, not records. Giving one a status would be the screen asserting something the system does not know.
D20
Dates are text. The row is the click target, and only when it is a real visit.
Stated requirement, and the thing that stops the list drifting back into being a calendar.
D21
Read surface, with exactly one action: the Unassigned pill.
Scanning speed is the feature. But unrouted demand you can see and cannot fix is the original complaint in a new costume.
D22
The period roll-up shows only companies with visits in the period. Quiet companies stay in the rail, dimmed.
Reverses 06 D10’s default for this surface. On the planning grid a quiet row earns its space by carrying a next-due date; here the period is the question, and the rail plus search is the way to anyone else.
D23
Overdue and missed visits pin above the roll-up in their own band.
Same device as the grid’s unassigned band. A missed visit outlives its date (06 D5) and is the one thing on this screen worth interrupting for.
D24
The view is deep-linkable: scope, company and property live in the URL.
Reverses session 3B’s “no URL state”. “Send me Downtown Holdings’ schedule” is the use case, and a link is the cheapest possible answer to it.
Edge cases

What breaks it, and what each case does

CaseBehaviour
Property with no recurring service Amber Not scheduled, sorted to the top of its company block, with the last visit date beneath it. Same treatment as the grid (06 D8) so the fact reads identically in both places.
Every property in a company unscheduled The company shows in the rail with an amber count of zero and its year page states the whole condition once, at the top, rather than repeating it per property.
One property, two contracts Two blocks, not one merged block — a rooftop AHU service on 90 days and a filter programme on 30 days are two arrangements with two cadences. Merging them would invent an interval nobody sold.
Cadence changed mid-year Planned visits stand; projection recomputes from the new interval and anchor. The block states Interval changed 12 Jun · every 60 days so the gap in the dates is explained rather than looking like a bug.
Contract ends inside the horizon Projection stops at the end date and the block says so: Contract ends 31 Mar 2027. Projecting past a contract invents work that has not been sold.
Harmonize moves a visit The list shows the new date immediately — one dataset, and this is the payoff for that. The projection re-anchors on the moved visit, so the following dates shift too.
Missed visit still open Appears in the overdue band of every period until it is rescheduled or cancelled, and in the company year in the alert colour above the upcoming dates.
Cancelled visit Struck through and greyed in the company year if it was planned. A projected date is never “cancelled” — it just stops being projected.
Two visits, one property, one day Two rows. The time window is the disambiguator and it is already a column; collapsing them would hide the second visit’s route.
Property outside the user’s zone or franchise Excluded — and the company header count must be the count of what is shown. “5 properties” over three rows is a support ticket.
Company renamed Nothing breaks: the key is customerId, the label is read fresh. A deep link stays valid.
Long book The rail is paged and searchable; the pane virtualizes. 300 companies × 12 months is a server query, not a client loop — see below.
Timezone Every date renders in the franchise timezone, as the grid does. Note handoff §7: the mock’s non-visit times still carry a UTC skew, so a list built off them will read wrong before the visit windows are used.
Search matches a property, not a company The company block still frames the result — you get the company, with non-matching properties collapsed behind +3 other properties. The hierarchy is never broken by a filter.
Nothing in the period at all An empty state that names the next date something happens, and who it is for. Never a blank pane.
Signal tenant The tab does not appear. Signal has visits and would pass the Visits tab’s own gate, so this needs a flag of its own: daily guarding has no cadence, and a twelve-month view of a site visited every day is twelve months of noise. The gate is the shape of the service, never the tenant’s name — a Signal client on a monthly patrol contract should get it.
Data

What the backend has to supply

Three reads, all server-side. The projection in particular must not be a client loop — twelve months of cadence across a full book is exactly the arithmetic a database does well and a browser does badly, and doing it on the client would also put the horizon boundary in the wrong place.

EndpointReturns
GET /visits/by-company?from&to The period roll-up. Companies with visits in the range, each with its properties and their visits — id, date, window, cadence, route, technician, status. Plus a roll-up of counts for the header, computed on the same filter as the rows.
GET /companies/{customerId}/schedule?months=12 Per property: address, contract, cadence, lastVisit, the planned visits, the projected dates, contractEndsAt where one exists, and scheduledHorizonEndsAt — the date the dashed line is drawn on.
GET /companies?query=&page= The rail. Name, customerId, property count, and the count of visits in the current period so the rail and the pane cannot disagree.

Fields that do not exist yet

  • cadence per property (or per contract) — interval and anchor. The one hard dependency; see the honesty rule above.
  • scheduledHorizonEndsAt — how far ahead visits are actually generated. Without it every date has to be labelled projected, which is honest but much less useful.
  • customerId on the visit’s site, reliably. It exists on the site record today and is already used by billing; it needs to survive into the visits payload.
  • contractEndsAt — optional, but without it the projection will happily schedule work beyond the end of a contract.

For the demo

Everything needed is already in schedule.mock.js, which is a strong signal that this is buildable rather than aspirational: the book is a cadence (VISIT_SITE_BOOK, 46 sites on 14–120 day intervals with two unscheduled), and isSiteDueOn / nextDueDayIndex already are the projection function. Two additions:

  • A company column on the book, many-to-one — today all 46 sites are effectively standalone, and a company view of 46 companies with one property each would demo the layout while hiding the point. Aim for ~18 companies, a few with four or five properties, one with a single property, one with a property that has no cadence.
  • A synthetic horizon — say 21 days — so the planned/projected boundary is visible in the demo rather than theoretical.
Sequence

Build order

First
The tab, and the period roll-up on existing data. A companies tab config, then the visits the week grid already holds, grouped by company, in a list. No projection, no rail selection — a scope control, company groups, rows and the overdue band. This alone answers “who are we visiting this week” and is the half that needs no backend change.
Second
The rail and the company year. Company list, selection, the twelve-month projection with the planned/projected boundary, the property filter, the twelve-row cap. This is the half that answers Umair’s actual objection, and the half that needs the cadence field.
Third
The edges. Deep links, CSV export, search across both levels, the empty and amber states, and the multi-contract case. Individually small; collectively the difference between a demo and a screen somebody uses on the phone to a customer.
Demo before build The last version of this was rejected in one sentence at the demo. The first phase is deliberately the one that runs on data that already exists — build it, show it against the real book, and get the grouping and the row shape agreed before the projection work starts.
Still open

Questions this design creates

  1. Is the contracted service interval stored per property? Promoted from “needed for compliance” to blocking — the twelve-month view is arithmetic on this field. If it does not exist, the fallback is inferring the interval from the last three visits, which is wrong the first time a customer changes their contract.
  2. How far ahead does the scheduler actually generate visits? Decides where the planned/projected line falls. A fixed rolling window, a per-franchise setting, or as-far-as-someone-has-built?
  3. Is customerId on the site authoritative and unique? Billing treats it as the customer key. Can one property belong to two companies — a landlord and a managing agent — and if so, which one owns the row?
  4. Cadence per property, or per contract? The invoice model already gives a site multiple contracts. The design assumes per contract and draws a block each; confirm before the field is named.
  5. Is CSV enough, or does the franchisee need a branded customer-facing PDF? The export exists because this list gets read to customers. A PDF is a different piece of work and should be scoped separately if it is wanted.
  6. Calendar month or rolling 30 days for “this month”? Recommendation: calendar month, because that is how the question is asked. The range text makes it unambiguous either way.
  7. Does “company” need a tenant label? The screen says Company; the data says customerId, company and client in three different places. One term should win, and it should go through getLabel like every other tenant noun.
  8. Should the roll-up scope include a “next 30 days”? Not designed in — three scopes is already the requirement’s answer — but a franchisee planning stock might want it. Worth asking before it is added on someone’s hunch.
  9. What turns the Companies tab on? The Visits tab’s gate lets Signal through, and daily guarding has nothing to project. The design assumes a service-level flag — on for interval-based service, off for daily — rather than a tenant check, so a Signal client on a monthly patrol contract is not excluded by accident. Confirm the flag exists, or name what should stand in for it.
